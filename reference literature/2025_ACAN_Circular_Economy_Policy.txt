The need for better circular economy policy:
The construction industry requires stronger regulations to integrate circular economy principles throughout the entire lifecycle of 
projects, not just at the planning stage. Currently, circularity efforts often focus on early design phases, but without enforcement 
mechanisms during construction, operation, and end-of-life stages, these principles fail to drive meaningful change. Planning 
regulations have been an invaluable tool in moving the needle towards circularity, but to further support this transition, we need 
clear and ambitious national regulations.

Embedding circular economy requirements into building regulations and building control systems would ensure accountability 
by mandating the use of sustainable materials, modular construction techniques, and effective waste management strategies. 
A more structured regulatory framework is necessary to create incentives for businesses to adopt circular practices and to move 
away from the traditional linear “take-make-dispose” model that currently dominates the industry.

Beyond regulatory changes, industry-wide collaboration is crucial to making circular construction the norm. Developers, investors, 
design teams, contractors, and policymakers must work together to implement standardised approaches that prioritise material 
reuse, adaptability, and low-carbon construction techniques. By requiring life cycle assessments, digital material passports, and 
deconstruction strategies in regulations, authorities can steer the sector towards greater resource efficiency. Without these 
structural changes, sustainability efforts will remain fragmented and voluntary, limiting their impact. Only a comprehensive, well-
enforced regulatory approach can drive the widespread adoption of circular economy principles and create a truly sustainable built 
environment.

Overall demands:

1.- The Environment Act:
•	 Makes manufacturers responsible for their products along the entire lifecycle (more take-back schemes)
•	 Extended producer responsibility for construction materials
•	 Powers to introduce already exist in the Environment Act and will be applied to packaging from this year
•	 By placing additional costs on raw materials, encourages a reduction in their use
•	 Key to successful EPR-free modulation. Can be used to incentivise different behaviors to minimize ecological impact e.g., 

products designed for recyclability/reusability could attract a lower fee

2.- Amend Existing Regulations
•	 Part A (Structure): Introduce durability and adaptability requirements to extend building lifespans.
•	 Part D (Toxic Substances): Ensure non-toxic, recyclable materials are prioritized.
•	 Waste and Resource Efficiency (WRAP) Standards: Align with BS 8001 (Circular Economy) and require a Material Passports 

system

3.- Create new regulations:
•	 Part Z approval with an introduction of new Circular Economy regulations, or embed circular economy targets or principles in 

Part Z 
•	 Introduce New Circular Economy Regulations, or embed circular economy targets or principles in Part Z
•	 Mandatory Whole Life Carbon Assessments (like in London Plan 2021), with a project tracker that would track progress during 

all design stages
•	 Deconstruction and Reuse Plans: Require new buildings or building elements to be designed for disassembly and material 

recovery.
•	 End-of-life Responsibility: Developers must ensure materials can be reused or recycled post-demolition.
•	 Mandating Recycled Content: Require a minimum percentage of recycled materials in new construction projects.
•	 Demolition & Waste Management Regulations: Require selective demolition to facilitate material recovery.
•	 Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR): Hold manufacturers accountable for the end-of-life impact of their materials
•	 Mandatory materials passport aligns with the EU’s Digital Product Passport (DPP)
•	 Align with ISO 20887 (Design for Disassembly and Adaptability)
•	 Building Code Adaptations: Adjust regulations to make retrofitting easier by allowing for the reuse of materials and 

components.
•	 Mandatory Building Assessments: Require lifecycle assessments for older buildings to determine if retrofit is a viable 

alternative to demolition. Refer to Pre-redevelopment audits as required by London Plan
•	 Reuse and Adaptation Guidelines: Develop clear standards for reusing structural components, such as steel beams, bricks, and 

wood, in retrofit projects.
•	 Public Retrofit Targets: Set government-led targets for retrofitting public buildings instead of demolishing them, setting an 

example for private developers.
•	 By promoting retrofit-first approaches, policymakers can significantly reduce construction waste and the demand for virgin 

materials, making the industry more circular.

4.- Strengthen Planning Policies (National & Local Level)
•	 Require a circular economy statement and pre-demolition audit as part of the planning application (Already required by the 

Mayor of London for referable schemes and some major projects depending on the borough). This could be part of the National 
Planning Policy Framework

•	 Update National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) to embed CE principles, and ask for pre-demolition audits and circular 
economy statements on a national level

•	 Local councils should incentivise reused materials via tax benefits or planning permissions.
•	 The policy should require projects to follow the mitigation hierarchy based on the UKGBC’s guidance



5.- Circular Economy statement:
•	 Keeping track of the percentage of the existing building that will be retained and reused is important during all the design 

stages. Tools embedded into Building Control environment. 
•	 How we measure circular economy needs to be project-specific, but there was a clear agreement to add carbon and 

environmental metrics
•	 The current planning systems can be improved to better encourage circular economy practices. Embedding targets and 

requirements to discharge at PC to make sure that the circular economy principles follow through all design stages  
•	 Aspiration for a government-supported database for building information. Even though the planning portal fulfills this duty for 

early stages, it would be good to have something similar for later design stages. Localised database of arisings from Pre-demo 
audits with contact details to enable material harvesting between projects

•	 Clear guidance on what the penalty could be for not meeting targets set out in a planning CE statement

6.- Pre-demolition audits:
•	 Commissioning a pre-demolition audit should be a planning requirements for all projects
•	 It is important to run a desk study and design team meeting before visiting the building to not only understand and investigate 

the existing building but also for the team running the audit to understand what the design team envisions for the building
•	 There is a good use for pre-demolition audits at later design stages to develop the strip-out scope, confirm reuse predictions, 
•	 It is important to add the pre-demolition audit as part of the tender package to make sure the contractor uses this information 
•	 The PDA is used as the basis/brief for demolition. It is the catalogue of those materials (rather than the quantities) that are 

used in the subsequent stages of design development

7.- Financial and Market Incentives
•	 Introduce tax reductions for developers using reclaimed materials at the national level.
•	 Material Exchange Platforms: Government-backed digital platforms for trading surplus materials.
•	 Green Bonds and Subsidies for projects adopting circular construction methods.
•	 Retrofit Incentives & Grants: Offer tax credits, low-interest loans, or direct grants for upgrading existing buildings to improve 

energy efficiency and reduce material use.

8.- Public Procurement Policies
•	 Sustainable Procurement Standards: Government-funded projects should prioritise circular materials and designs.
•	 Lifecycle Costing in Contracts: Require project bids to consider long-term material reuse and recyclability.
•	 Establish an incentive for demolishing contractors to provide deconstruction services at reasonable cost 

9.- Innovation & Research Policies
•	 Funding for physical infrastructure - national network of reuse hubs for storing, testing, reconditioning and upcycling of 

reclaimed materials
•	 Funding for Circular Economy Technologies: Support R&D for sustainable construction methods, such as 3D printing with 

recycled materials.
•	 Knowledge Sharing Platforms: Create databases for secondary materials and best practices.

10.- Industry Collaboration & Education
•	 Establish clear Circular Economy guidelines for architects, engineers, and contractors.
•	 Invest in CE-focused training programs and R&D for sustainable materials.
•	 Teach Circular economy principles at the university level, to make sure that the new generation of designers will know how to 

embed circularity into their buildings
•	 Training Programs for Professionals: Educate architects, engineers, and construction workers on circular economy principles.
•	 Public Awareness Campaigns: Promote the benefits of circular construction to developers and consumers.
