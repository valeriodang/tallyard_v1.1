Executive Summary 

In this report, the CIRCuIT partner cities Copenhagen, Helsinki region, Hamburg and London 

share learnings and best practice after conducting pre-demolition audits (PDA) in eight 

demolition demonstrator projects (two in each partner city). Through the demolition 

demonstrators, partners showcased the technical feasibility of selective demolition techniques 

to preserve building elements and materials for reuse and recycling. Concurrently, a CIRCuIT 

pre-demolition audit template was created and refined. 

Helsinki region showcased the selective disassembly of whole bricks. Freeze-thaw 

resistance tests were conducted, with results showing that hollow bricks maintain the least 

amount of freeze-thaw resistance over the years in use. Key limitations for the reuse of bricks 

were concluded to be time (disassembly and cleaning) and offsite storage due to reuse 

schedules. It was concluded that systematic and timely pre-demolition audits need to be 

carried out prior to demolitions to optimise the recovery rate.  

Copenhagen showcased the selective disassembly of wooden roof elements, bricks, steel 

cladding and roof tiles. The importance of expert reuse consultants in the initial mapping of 

the building and steering cross-disciplinary processes was found to be crucial in transforming 

linear building traditions into circular practices. The pre-demolition audit, the procurement 

process and project tenders incorporating the reuse of building materials were found to be 

critical to increase project material reuse.  

Hamburg showcased the recycling of mixed construction and demolition waste for use as 

aggregate in recycled concrete. Mixed construction and demolition waste was found to be the 

largest waste stream by mass in both demonstrator demolitions. Key limitations included the 

difficulty of material separation, in particular the presence of mortar, dusts containing mortar 

and gypsum and un-hydrated cement. Key recommendations included improved separation 

techniques including separating by concrete class.  

London showcased innovative demolition techniques including crushing and recycling 2,800 

m3 bulk materials onsite as basement fill. Information collected from two sites contributing to 

London demonstrator 3 showed that it did not take longer to remove timber material than it 

normally would have. Two important areas for development of the sector were identified to be 

better building information (digitised) and a well-functioning reuse market/network. 

Key findings learnt regarding pre-demolition audits include that: 

• The level of detail of reporting must shift from a hazardous-material focused PDA to a PDA 

focused on reusable and recyclable elements and components in order to facilitate 

recirculation. The CIRCuIT PDA template seeks to improve this. 

• PDAs should be conducted regardless the building demolition contract. Ideally, the PDA should 

be conducted when the building risk of demolition is increasing, thus to overcome timing issues,

and to generate a comprehensive and complete building passport and/or expected resource

• Cities can improve reused/recycled material circulation with mandatory audits for all size of

• Circular outcomes increase when key project stakeholders are involved early via a participatory

• The effect is higher when outcomes of the PDA are used to establish circular targets with a

Key findings learnt regarding demolition that furthers circularity include: 

• Industry training to improve selective demolition techniques and technology use for damage 

free disassembly. 

• The margins for improvement in selective demotion technologies and workforce skills are large 

and have a direct impact on material qualities (function, strength or appearance). 

• Material storage is a common limiting factor during demolition activities. 

• Cities can improve demolition for circularity through policies that facilitate more material storage 

facilities (either directly via providing facilities or indirectly through planning/economic 

incentives). 

• Project leaders are important to ensure that material storage and recirculation planning are 

taken into account already at early planning stages to avoid transportation, with onsite storage 

and onsite reuse/recycling where possible.  

Key findings learnt regarding material circularity include: 

• Improved data transparency and material information, which the CIRCuIT pre-demolition audit 

contributes to with increased reporting detail regarding elements, components and materials. 

Improved material information improves market confidence and provides opportunities for long 

term planning. Improved material information should facilitate quality assurance and in turn 

provide consumers with more product choices that compete with virgin construction materials 

and enable more opportunities for closed loop reuse and recycling of materials. 

• Incentives are required to facilitate reuse and high-value recycling. As learnt from the CIRCuIT 

stakeholder engagement activities, extra effort is expected during highly selective demolition 

activities and material preparation for reuse. Therefore, an attractive reclaimed materials 

market and circularity frameworks should not be undervalued in future recommendations. 

• There is a growing need and demand for centralised platforms for the industry to share, 

network, offer, and exchange materials to facilitate reuse/recycling.

1. Introduction 

This report investigates the pre-demolition audit (PDA) process in CIRCuIT partner cities. 

The aim is to present lessons learnt to identify options to improve the reuse and 

recycling performance in cities.  

In the framework of CIRCuIT, the partners conducted market surveys and regional workshop 

in order to identify user requirements for reclaimed building materials and framework for 

material circularity. The results show that reused/recycled material users typically require 

highly detailed product information for use in construction projects, whereas reporting 

of material arising from demolitions is typically not reported with enough detail. A PDA 

is a driver to improve known information about elements embedded in a building (European 

Commission 2018). 

Theoretical lessons learnt are based on existing PDA policy in EU Member States (chapter 2) 

and stakeholder engagement in partner cities (chapter 3). Practical lessons learnt are based 

on inventory assessments and pre-demolition audits in eight actual demolitions and PDAs 

(chapter 5).  

The main practical outcome of the report is an improved CIRCuIT PDA template and 

checklist. This, along with lessons learnt presented, is intended as a direction to assist built 

environment professionals planning demolitions and material recirculation in the future. 

Existing demolition auditing methodologies typically focus on hazardous materials and their 

waste codes and are time constrained. Such methodologies require innovation in order to 

improve the identification of reusable components in the early stages of demolitions. Common 

reusable components of interest include bricks, tiles and cladding in addition to recyclable 

materials such as wood and concrete. 

This report documents pre-demolition audits conducted by the CIRCuIT partner cities. 

Helsinki region and Copenhagen city clusters aimed to enable reuse of building 

components through their demolition demonstrators. Hamburg and London city clusters 

aimed to enable the recycling of building materials through their demolition demonstrators. 

A CIRCuIT pre-demolition audit template is provided in the appendix, encompassing the 

lessons learnt from the demonstrator projects.

2. Evaluation of pre-demolition audit guidelines 

This section summarises the status quo of pre-demolition audits in Europe including existing 

guidelines and practice.  

Pre-demolition audit reporting is a driver to improve the recovery or materials and components 

from demolitions for subsequent high value applications (European Commission 2018). The 

potential to increase circularity is high, since a relevant quota of recovered demolition waste 

is only backfilled/downgraded (Eurostat and European Commission 2018, own calculations). 

However best practice currently varies across Europe. Pre-demolition audit is an essential first 

step towards recycling and appropriate Construction and Demolition (C&D) waste 

management (European Commission 2018; Tecnalia - VTT - RPA 2016). The aim of this first 

step is the collection of information about elements, quantities, and possible treatment paths 

of building materials. 

The level of monitoring (mandatory vs. voluntary vs. incomplete data) varies throughout 

Europe. The majority of European countries apply waste audits only in specific cases, i.e. 

some building types/hazardous materials or without considering furniture and installations. A 

suggested innovation from CIRCuIT is to extend the materials and products assessment prior 

to demolition to all types of buildings and infrastructures without size thresholds, to enable the 

assessment of more reusable elements.  

Furthermore, data quality is influenced by differing monitoring techniques and by the unclear 

definition of backfilling (European Commission. Directorate General for Internal Market, 

Industry, Entrepreneurship and SMEs. 2018). This is an important aspect for countries where 

the recovery rate depends on the backfilling rate. This highlights the need for clear definitions 

of C&D waste and backfilling. 

 

 European Waste Audit Guideline  

In 2018, the European Commission published the European Waste Audit Guideline (European 

Commission 2018), based on the DG Grow - Directorate-General for Internal Market, Industry, 

Entrepreneurship and SMEs final report 2016 (Tecnalia - VTT - RPA 2016). This Guideline 

provides information about the appropriate way to structure the “waste audit”, based on the 

evaluation of C&D waste flows deriving from demolition, deconstruction and renovation of a 

building or infrastructure. Figure 1 on the following page represents the recommended scheme 

for the pre-demolition audit process. 

The different steps in the scheme are briefly presented in the following subsections.

Desk Study 

The desk study involves analysing existing building documentation. The aim is to collect 

information about the history of the “target” building (technical drawings, maintenance and 

renovation documents), as well as information about the type of materials and 

construction techniques used, the location etc. The age of building and/or past 

refurbishment activities are essential information, also related to the presence or not of 

hazardous materials. Swedish and Austrian guidelines provide a list of structural and non-

structural elements that can contain hazardous substances. This may be an aid to evaluate 

and quantify the hazardous elements found on site and to prevent safety issues during the 

field survey. The access and surroundings may give further information about the conditions 

for storage, transport and management of the waste streams. 

Field Survey 

The field survey involves the visual inspection of the site to be demolished. In this phase, the 

experience of the auditors is essential to collect data useful for the inventory. Sampling and 

laboratory analyses are requested for suspected hazardous materials. This phase is important 

to quantify the products and furniture, to evaluate their condition and possibility to be reused 

and to estimate the amount of waste streams arising from the building demolition. During the 

site visit, it is necessary to evaluate the nature and amount of materials through non-

destructive and destructive techniques. In addition, to ensure consistency between the data 

retrieved during the desk study and site visit, a good practice is to take photos and/or 

measurements of materials that will be considered in the final inventory.

Inventory 

The inventory is based on the desk study and field survey and concerns the material and 

element assessment, in order to decide the best waste management operation. It includes the 

type and quantification of waste arising from demolition, the European waste code and 

description. This information should be supplemented by photos and comments that allow an 

easier interpretation of the audit. It is important to extend this study to all levels of the building, 

to understand the location of each type of material. 

The inventory is strongly linked to the identification of hazardous materials. As such, the 

identification of non-hazardous materials is often seen through a hazardous materials lens 

and often lacks detailed information useful to recirculate components and materials.  

Management recommendations 

The waste audit can be completed with recommendations on how to perform waste 

management on site. The final report must summarize the information collected during the 

desk study and the site visit, and must present: (1) the scope and characteristics of project, 

site location and history, (2) the list of available documents, and the summary of 

recommendations, (3) the summary of waste audit and the explanation of techniques, the 

sampling and laboratory analysis used during the site visit, (4) the inventory of materials, the 

waste fraction arising from demolition, the list of hazardous waste and the description of 

precautionary measures to be applied, and (5) the inventory of elements, with reference to the 

quantity, quality conditions and potential reuse rate.  

The European Guidelines and DG GROW final report (Tecnalia - VTT - RPA 2016) provide 

templates that can be used to complete pre-demolition audits. The pre-demolition audit can 

be considered as a driver to improve data quality, legislation and market in the built 

environment, and therefore a necessary step to achieve a 70% recovery target according to 

the Waste Framework Directive (WFD). 

 Current C&D Waste Management Performance in EU Member States 

A desktop analysis was conducted to understand the existing C&D waste auditing and 

management practices across Europe.

materials up to 70% by weight. In 2016, according to waste statistics (Eurostat 2021), only two 

countries did not reach this target, whereas the European average value was 90% (Figure 2 

on the following page).

Backfilling 

The Directive (EU) 2018/8511 defines backfilling as “any recovery operation where suitable 

Non-Hazardous (NH) waste is used for purposes of reclamation in excavated areas or for 

engineering purposes in landscaping. Waste used for backfilling must substitute non-waste 

materials, be suitable for the aforementioned purposes, and be limited to the amount strictly 

necessary to achieve those purposes”. The C&D waste recovery target imposed by the WFD 

allows Member States to consider also backfilled waste into the computation of their national 

C&D waste recovery rate. However, some experts question this kind of recovery operation, 

justifying that there is an improper estimation of C&D waste backfilled data. The consequence 

is the lack of a harmonized application of this recovery method. Note also that the waste 

hierarchy defined by the WFD collocates recycling before backfilling and specifies that 

Member States should promote measures to obtain high-quality recycled materials.

the entity of treatment capacity of each Member State, and the presence of a robust market 

for recycled products. Scarcity of recycling facilities, infrastructures inadequacy along with 

environmental knowledge gaps, and lack of experience and competence obstacle the 

transition to zero waste. In addition, time, contractual requirements, cost of transport and cost 

to separate waste streams are other significant factors that may inhibit sorting demolition or 

reuse material.

2.3.2 Copenhagen  

The implementation of complete pre demolition audits is still happening on a voluntary basis 

in Denmark. It is however mandatory according to the Danish Waste regulation to perform a 

mandatory screening and/or audit for hazardous substances when renovating or demolishing 

a building. This rule applies to all projects that generate more than 1 Mg of waste and to all 

projects with windows that can have been produced in the period from 1950-1977.  

Except for the aforementioned screening, implementing a full, circular pre-demolition audit on 

building or renovations projects is not mandatory, and no regulations do yet demand building 

owners to perform a full pre demolition audit. However, The Danish Environmental Protection 

Agency has published a guideline and recommendations of selective demolition and pre-

demolition audit.3 Furthermore, in the Danish Strategy for Circular Economy4, which was 

published by the Danish Government September 2018, the goal is to promote selective 

demolition, including ways to make standardised demolition plans and training. The strategy 

is a first step in order to reach a higher level of reuse and recycling of building materials. 

The fact that pre-demolition audits happen on a voluntary basis means that these audits are 

typically performed by constructors or building owners with a strong sustainability strategy or 

brand. In the private building industry, performing circular pre-demolition audits is not yet a 

reality, but it is on its way. Different building certification systems such as DGNB also supports 

this process. Several larger pension companies in Denmark apply the DGNB-certification 

system to their building projects which rewards performing pre-demolition audits. The different 

building certification schemes such as DGNB, LEED and BREEAM thus plays an important 

role in increasing circularity within construction. The number of certified buildings in Denmark 

increased from 7 % in 2017, 15 % in 2018 and 23 % in 2019.5 

However, the pre-demolition audit itself is not enough. Since it adds cost to the building or 

demolition project, there must be a market for circulating the building materials. It is essential 

to support industry to increase the demand for more reused building materials in upcoming 

projects and get the loop started.

2.3.4 London 

A key driver for pre-demolition audits is BREEAM; the Building Research Establishment’s 

(BRE) Environmental Assessment Method which sets best practice standards for the 

environmental performance of buildings through design, specification, construction and 

operation. An audit has to be undertaken to obtain credits relating to demolition waste 

management. This helped to highlight that there had been little guidance available about how 

to conduct audits, which had implications for the quality of audit work and the potential for 

commercial interests to influence audit outcomes. In 2017, BRE published a ‘Code of Practice’ 

for pre-redevelopment audits to help provide clearer guidance on the requirements of an audit 

and the procedures to follow. 

In 2021, the CIRCuIT London cluster engaged multiple stakeholders (through surveys, 

interviews and workshops) from both the demand and supply side of construction material 

reuse and high-value recycling in London. Some of the key barriers found that impact the 

incorporation of pre-demolition audits included lack of reuse/ higher value recycling aspirations 

among clients (developers & investors) and a lack of legislative measures. There were 

typically no set targets for reuse within demolition projects. When clients were motivated, the 

highlighted barriers were either supply and demand gaps in the market or challenges with 

segregating materials due to the way contemporary materials are designed. There is a more 

established network for the recycling of all building materials than for reuse/reclamation. 

Several respondents also commented on the industry’s lack of awareness and skills to conduct 

proper on-site segregation and lack of knowledge on reuse/higher value recycling. For 

example, most of the demolition contractors consider reprocessing or downcycling as reuse 

(more details on findings in 3.4).

requires referable applications (for planning permission of potential strategic importance) to 

promote circular economy outcomes and aim to be net zero-waste (City of London 2021).A

 Pre-demolition Audit Best Practice Summary 

According to the European guidelines and CIRCuIT partners (see next chapter), the following 

points have been identified.  

The European guidelines for PDA (European Commission 2018) state that an audit must 

contain: 

• Information regarding building ownership, building location and other details useful for the 

planning of the demolition activities (e.g. found via desk study) 

• Inventory of hazardous substances, materials and components required by authorities 

• Indication on management of the identifies materials (e.g. reuse, recycle, backfill, energy 

recovery, disposal) 

Best practice pre-demolition audits provide a holistic approach for the identification and 
management of in use building materials. 
 
A pre-demolition audit should also contain: 

• Elements, components and materials that do not become waste  

• Material reclamation advice  

• Waste management recommendations and potential waste management contractors 

• Any other relevant information given the site context and/or project requirements   

Other areas often lacking consideration in current PDA auditing schemes include: 

• Scope of application of pre-demolition audits - applying the pre-demolition audit to all type of 

buildings and infrastructures without size thresholds.  

• Project timeline for pre-demolition audit – the pre-demolition audit should be a planning tool 

implemented as early as possible to assist with evaluation of refurbishment as an alternative 

option to demolition and to avoid vandalism or material quality deterioration prior to extraction. 

• Creation of more opportunities for closed loop reuse and recycling of materials, especially reuse 

of materials from the demolition/refurbishment process in new construction works.

 Copenhagen  

Through stakeholder engagement activities, the Copenhagen partners have identified several 

important factors to reach a high level of reuse and recycling of building materials.  

A key theme is partnerships across actors in the building sector. Many have considerable 

knowledge on circular building principles but sharing them through partnerships is key to 

improving the circular marketplace as well as improving a common understanding of the 

principles and their application. 

The need to rethink and adapt conventional economic tools to calculate, measure and 

evaluate the value of circular buildings was identified. One tool could be to include CO₂ 
as a parameter, e.g. through a shadow price which is a theoretical price on carbon that can 

help prioritize low-carbon investments. 

New constellations of collaborations are needed. Examples could be inclusion of the 

demolisher in the design process, that the architect/designer takes part in the pre-demolition 

audit and the selective demolition process.  

To implement such new constellations and to allow more stakeholder engagement, it is 

needed to be able to document and test the materials earlier in the process. For example, 

including tests as part of the pre-demolition-audit, so that the risk of reusing/recycling the 

building component decreases, as we develop a great amount of knowledge in the early 

phases for decision making. 

It is required to increase transparency of data and knowledge for increasing demand on 

circular building practices. The lack of transparency and data makes it impossible for 

developers, architects, contractors, etc. to identify the circulation potential. Thus, the pre-

demolition-audit should support an increase in transparency of data.  

In general, there is great interest among stakeholders in circular economy and opportunities 

to increase the reuse of materials and building components from demolitions and major 

renovations. 

There are already a few material platforms and websites where reused materials can be 

purchased, and more are on the way due to an expected increased demand. This applies to 

both direct sales from the demolishers and platforms that act as "match making" for sell and 

buy. 

Right now, however, the industry is in a state of "waiting for each other". On the one hand 

designers/architects and clients are demanding a wider selection of building materials and 

components and the right quantities needed for the projects, and on the other hand e.g. the 

demolition companies (who can see a business opportunity) are asking for a greater demand. 

Establishing larger storage facilities is an investment and they need a return of investments. 

So, it is essential to support industry to increase the demand for more reused building

Reuse rates and target setting (phase 1) 

It was found that there are typically no set targets for reuse within demolition projects. 

Furthermore, interviews highlighted that most of the demolition contractors consider 

reprocessing or downcycling as reuse. For example, crushing of hardcore for use on-site or 

off-site is considered as reuse. This reflects an ambiguity in the understanding of reuse. 

Data captured from demolition and recycling contractors showed that reuse rates range from 

1% to 14% whereas data from reuse/reclamation contractors showed 18% to 95% reuse rates. 

The recycling rates captured from demolition and recycling contractors ranged from 55% to 

98%. 

Materials with higher reuse and recycling potential 

Demolition contractors (phase 1) highlighted raised floor tiles, timber/doors, metals, 

plasterboard/partitions, carpet, bricks(yellow)/stonework, and glass/partitions as the 

main building materials that have the potential to be carefully dismantled. Demand side (phase 

2) participants indicated that hardcore materials (such as bricks and stonework) and 

timber/wood have the most untapped potential for reuse. Timber/wood, 

plasterboard/gypsum and glass were voted as material streams that have the most 

untapped potential for recycling. 

Benefits and barriers 

Phase 1 of the investigation suggests that the key barriers to reuse for demolition, recycling 

and reuse/reclamation contractors are a lack of aspirations among clients (developers & 

investors) and a lack of legislative measures. In the instances where demolition contractors 

commended the client’s motivation for good practices, the highlighted barriers were either 

supply and demand gaps in the market or challenges with segregating materials due to 

the way contemporary materials are designed. A similar trend was found among reuse and 

recycling contractors. In addition to the above, several respondents commented on the 

industry’s lack of awareness and skills to conduct proper on-site segregation. 

Phase 2 results on barriers correlate well with phase 1. Developers/investors highlighted a 

lack of legislative measures. Designers expressed the challenge of clients not providing 

flexibility to design and both developers and designers indicated a lack of reuse/reclaim 

infrastructure as a barrier. Supply-chain challenges were further mentioned by consultants,

Recommendations for designers and developers 

• Early collaboration and audit - conduct a pre-demolition audit and involve demolition 

contractors at the early stages of development (ideally at the concept stage). 

• Improved material choices – consider emission reductions by first reducing the amount of 

material used. Additionally, design to avoid material contamination such as - painting/spraying 

materials with potentially harmful chemicals, etc. 

• Flexibility and adaptability – align considerations for reuse and high value recycling with the 

procurement process to give designers the opportunity to create flexible designs and 

incorporate reusable components. 

• Time and cost considerations – incorporate time and cost for facilitating reuse and high-value 

recycling as part of the tender process. 

Recommendations for the government 

• Mandates – introduce legal requirements to enforce reuse and high-value recycling. 

• Incentives – incentivise projects that facilitate reuse and high-value recycling. 

• Centralised system – provide a centralised platform for the industry to share, network, offer, 

and exchange materials for facilitating reuse.

5. Pre-demolition audit guidelines (CIRCuIT pre-demolition audit) 

This chapter describes the current version of the Pre-demolition audit (PDA) template adopted 

and enhanced during demonstrator activities. Subheadings mirror the structure of the PDA 

template and provide relevant results and observations from demolition demonstrators.  

The pre-demolition audit template is aligned with the EU guidelines document for pre-

demolition audit, the DG GROW framework document, the guidance document by the Finnish 

Ministry of the Environment, the Pre-redevelopment audit - Code of Practice by BRE, and the 

guidance document by PARADE project. 

 

 Demolition site framework: “integrated demolition” 

An aspect not often considered is the context of the demolition, or "demolition site framework”. 

In the desk study, information related to the target demolition building is collected. The early 

analysis of the target demolition building in context with the proposed design/future land use 

can help identify on-site preservation and reuse/recycling opportunities that are often missed

Often in standard pre-demolition auditing schemes, a general, high-level distinction between 

Hazardous (H) and Non-Hazardous (NH) materials is found. 

Information on the hazardousness/non-hazardousness of materials provides a continuity 

across different demolition projects and fulfils the requirements for CDW labelling and tracking. 

However, emphasising and focusing only on this information in the PDA reflects a cradle-to-

grave/linear demolition design process and ignores existing buildings as valuable material 

sources. 

In other words, distinguishing only between hazardous and non-hazardous waste ignores 

other properties of materials such as dimensions, function, quality, appearance, location, and 

historic/heritage value. 

The CIRCuIT project adopts a three-level identification of the building material stocks. The 

proposed building stock databank is divided into: 

• Hazardous materials 

• Non-Hazardous, reusable elements and components 

• Non-Hazardous, recyclable materials 

The three inventories can also be interpreted as a succession of phases involving different 

stakeholders: 

1. First, the evaluation of the hazardous materials is executed by the demolition 

contractor, environmental engineers, certified auditors and/or relevant local built 

environment professionals. 

2. Secondly, building developers, architects, engineers, planners, reuse operators and in 

general relevant built environment professionals, together with the demolition 

contractor, should compile the inventory of non-hazardous reusable elements and 

components. 

3. Lastly, once the auditing process has already excluded hazardous elements and the 

more valuable components, the identification of the remaining materials can be 

conducted by recycling experts and waste managers. The following subsections will 

present the proposed content for each inventory.

commonly performed as part of PDA schemes. The detection of hazardous elements, 

components and materials among the different CIRCuIT demonstrators was observed to be a

5.3.2 Inventory of Non-Hazardous, reusable elements and components 

The further analysis of the building stock continues with the identification of the non-

hazardous, reusable elements and components within the building. 

This step is normally performed by the auditor. The auditor role generally sits within the 

demolition contracting company. The auditor collects the information necessary to organize 

the demolition activities and to select the resources available for the task. 

However, the incomplete quantification and thorough documentation of materials and 

components often hampers the possible reuse of them in 1:1 applications. As a result, Building 

developers, architects and other relevant built environment professionals are often not aware 

of reusable materials and building components proximate to their project. For this reason, the 

inventory of non-hazardous, reusable element and components should be a participative 

process, in which architects, building developers, planners and all relevant project 

stakeholders are given the opportunity to provide audit input.

5.4.2 Copenhagen 

 

Selective demolition is already a well-integrated approach in Denmark and methods to take 

down materials for reuse and recycling are known and used by most demolishing companies.  

In a new agreement to reach a climate-neutral waste sector by 2030, the Danish government 

is introducing requirements for standardised demolition plans as well as competence 

requirements for selective demolition from 2023.  

Selective demolition can, however, not stand alone. The planning of the demolition and how 

to keep the building materials in the loop are important factors to reach an increased recycling 

and reuse of building materials from demolition projects. In order to get the most value out of 

the pre demolition audit, it is important to view the building materials as materials and not 

waste. The Copenhagen partners have experienced in their pre-demolition audit work that 

qualitative data and approaches are equally important as quantitative data.   

The pre-demolition audits is not just a tool to deliver data. The pre demolition audit when used 

the optimal way, is a valuable tool to ensure that building materials maintain or better their 

position in the waste hierarchy, as well as ensuring that all benefits from the building materials 

are gained. To display the value of the building materials, quantifying data is not enough. You 

need more qualitative and descriptive data such as compressive strength of bricks, tensile 

strength of wood and other properties of the building materials, but also more tactile and visual 

data or input to inspire architects and constructors know how to integrate the materials into 

new building projects. 

Working with the pre-demolition audits in the demonstrators has encouraged the Copenhagen 

partners to try out different methods of integrating the audit. The process has given insight in 

how the different method works and the ability to adapt the work according to the value it gives 

in the different construction projects. Enemærke & Petersen is integrating pre-demolition 

audits in their tender bids in projects, where the constructor requests suggestions on how

demolished materials can be reused. Enemærke & Petersen expects it to be a required

The pre demolition audit of a building ought to be a process where the stakeholders of the 

entire value chain are invited to a dialogue to ensure that the highest possible level of recycling 

and reuse, which was the case in both the Gladsaxe School and the Hyltebjerg School 

demonstrators. In that way, all stakeholders e.g., the developer, the architect, the demolition 

company, the engineering company and the waste handling company can inspect the building 

together and discuss the reuse and recycling potentials as well as potential users/buyers of 

the materials. The demolition company and the constructor are often not aware of the potential 

use value of the building materials in the building. Therefore, they are prone to choose a 

business-as-usual model, because time (and costs) often is an important and expensive factor 

when demolishing a building. The process and methods on how to integrate the value chain 

early in the process through an open house model, through a virtual open house or through a 

digital model should be investigated further. 

A lot of factors play a critical role in our ability to recirculate building materials: The age of the 

building, the building (dis)assembly, environmental and economic costs  Nonetheless, the 

processual nature of retrieving, storing, and processing the building materials is of critical 

concern, as is knowing which actors should be included throughout the different phases. Thus, 

the most optimal approach would be to have the demolisher and the architect/ developer to 

go through the building together to discuss/agree on reusable items.  

Another important finding in e.g. the Gladsaxe School demonstrator was the importance of 

expert consultants10, not only to do the initial mapping of the building, but also to be able to 

steer cross-disciplinary processes and act as an interpreter for the different stakeholders and 

professions and thus help transform linear building traditions into circular practices. Every 

stakeholder in a circular building project on both the demolition side and the construction side 

needs different knowledge at different times, and the expert consultants are valuable in this 

process in order to connect the knowledge and have the overview of the entire process. While 

developing material knowledge on both a technical and processual level, the consultant can 

facilitate communication or transfer of knowledge across the project collaborators. 

As an example, the timber construction from the roof in the Gladsaxe School demonstrator 

(Figure 37) had to be cut free in a way that you could retain certain dimensions for the new 

construction project. The timber was therefore cut free according to predefined and agreed 

upon cutting lines. The timber then had to be stored in a place where it was covered and 

ventilated, so it did not rot. A close cooperation between collaborators in both ends of the 

value chain of the project and the expert consulting was important for this process to succeed.

demolition. London demonstrator 2 also had steel barriers and staircase railings that were in 

good condition for reuse but these were sent to metal recycling. The contractor highlighted 

that there are challenges with bespoke designs that require the off-taker to need very similar 

dimensions and there is a lack of storage for high quantities. Interestingly, a similar challenge 

occurred with London demonstrator 3 in the reverse scenario. The target source of materials 

for London demonstrator 3 was stud work from internal partitions; however, as sourcing was 

limited to the available timber from the demolition sites, the timber received was from raised 

floors and false ceilings. 

For London demonstrator 1, the structural frame was originally designed for adaptability. It is 

modular and most of its members are assembled by mechanical means. Corrugated 

aluminium panels clad both the roof and facade and are mechanically fixed to the sub-

structure through fasteners. Four options were identified for metal reuse/recycling: 

• Relocate a large single component into another product with little refurbishment, such as 

cleaning and simple repairs/adjustments. This usually happens when demand for the service 

provided is high and the component is in a good condition.

• Cascade to a less demanding use, when both demand is low and the condition is poor or 

unknown. 

• Reshape to form a new, more useful, geometry. 

• Remanufacture, entailing further disassembly (of a subassembly), redrilling, and metallic 

spraying/thermal techniques to recover worn and fatigued surfaces. This is recommended 

when demand exists but the component has suffered significant degradation, or an upgrade is 

needed. 

Relocation and remanufacture are the most effective strategies as they maintain the value of 

the component in its second use. Relocation could be done at building, component or element 

scale. Reforming and cascading typically apply when a downgrading of required properties is 

acceptable.

For the remanufacturing process, the manufacturer advised that longer lengths are preferable

Hazardous 

For London demonstrator 2, a refurbishment survey for asbestos-containing materials (ACMs) 

was conducted to plan for the removal of all ACMs as far as reasonably practicable from the 

premises prior to demolition. The survey records the location, extent and product type of any 

presumed or known ACMs. The asbestos type is determined by collecting representative 

samples of suspect materials for laboratory identification or by making an assumption based 

on the product type and its appearance, etc. A waste management contractor that is licensed 

for hazardous waste should be contacted to manage these substances and waste must be 

handled and managed according to requirements. The Construction (Design and 

Management) Regulations require a Health and Safety File to be available for maintenance 

contractors and designers and contractors preparing or carrying out future construction work. 

Any works that are carried out on the structure or its equipment that effect the file should be 

recorded. The file helps to enable safe working practices. A Health and Safety File update for 

London demonstrator 1 was prepared after removal of the ACMs. 

The identified ACMs were mastics and gaskets found throughout the site. Asbestos containing 

mastic was identified from the older riveted ventilation ducting that was largely found in risers. 

It was not reasonably practicable during the survey to fully trace the ducting as it left the risers 

into ceiling voids. It was, therefore, recommended that all mastic to the older rectangular 

ducting be removed and disposed of as asbestos waste. The presence of asbestos fibre 

contained in mastic is a major concern. Asbestos was used as a bonding agent in the mastic 

and the asbestos fibres would not easily be seen. The fibres can become airborne and inhaled 

when disturbed by drilling, hammering, scraping, etc. or due to deterioration. A key identifier 

for intervention is if the age of the building where the mastic is found pre-dates the 1980s. 

Mastic is similar in appearance to silicone sealant and can be visually identified by its colour; 

usually a dull grey/green to black. It was used in similar situations as silicone may now be 

used. Typical places to find this material are: between the frames of air-conditioning ducts; as 

'gap filler' around pipework through walls, and; on the external face of concrete beams and 

slabs that may be drilled into. The mastic was originally used in the formwork to prevent slurry 

seeping and was left behind in the stripping process (Health and Safety Executive 2021).

Legislation 

London Plan, which is the Spatial Development Strategy for Greater London, Policy 5.16 sets

Sustainability champions 

The sourcing of secondary timber from demolition sites presented a range of problems. Initially 

it was difficult to find projects that were being demolished and get commitments from willing 

contractors. Eventually two contractors expressed interest and within both of these 

organisations it was the sustainability manager driving the process and trying to get 

cooperation from their site teams. The demolition contractor for London demonstrator 1 has 

introduced a new 5-year strategy that involves the roll out of new commitments such as 

offering deconstruction or a more circular alternative for tenders and setting Science Based 

Targets for carbon reduction. Typically, alternative offers are focussed on lower cost, less 

time, purchasing the asset, etc. but this is one of the contractor’s approaches to introducing 

circular economy thinking amongst clients. These alternatives would still be compliant with the 

tender requirements (programme costs and time etc.). Reuse and the circular economy are 

increasingly being discussed and this is seen as an important area for the company to position 

itself in. There is ongoing internal training and the contractor has been involved with projects 

to improve the separation and removal of glass as it typically ends up downcycled. 

Future outlook 

Higher level recycling could potentially reduce recycling and landfill costs but there are 

limitations with material quality, time to deconstruct, storage, etc. Sometimes the quantity of 

material is too large and there is no storage on site. Off-takers are also worried about product 

quality, warranties, etc. so there is not enough demand for certain materials. For London 

demonstrator 2 contractor, two of the most important areas for development of the sector are 

better building information (digitized) and a well-functioning reuse market/network.

 Conclusion to Chapter 5 

 

This chapter presented the results of the development of the CIRCuIT PDA template. From 

one side, the key lesson learnt during the development of the framework is that the pre-

demolition audit should not be limited to the sole identification of different waste fractions and 

hazardous materials, but to include extra-effort for the identification of elements and 

components considering and providing an additional level of information required to assess 

their reutilisation in new structures. 

Therefore, detailed information about elements and components dimensions become as 

important as common information about expected material amounts, as well as information 

about material quality, extractability and market value which are expected to positively affect 

the interest into reusable building materials. 

However, the abovementioned efforts should be sustained by a favourable framework such 

as the “integrated demolition”, in which the identified materials are directly linked to their further 

use in the building-to-be-developed (direct reuse), or functional material exchange portals, 

where these information can be used to advertise the reclaimed building element or 

component. 

These aspects, and the key findings from the previous chapters will be summarised in the next 

conclusive chapter to this report.

6. Conclusions 

This report has presented auditing and demolition techniques to preserve building elements 

and materials for reuse and recycling in eight actual demolition demonstrators (two in each 

CIRCuIT city cluster). All city clusters demonstrated innovative selective demolition techniques 

and/or material processing techniques enabling reuse or recycling opportunities. The Helsinki 

region and Copenhagen city clusters focused on the reuse of building components with their 

demolition demonstrators. The Hamburg and London city clusters focused on the recycling of 

building materials with their demolition demonstrators.  

A theoretical perspective on the strengths and weaknesses of existing pre-demolition audit 

practice in EU Member States was presented in chapter 2, along with existing PDA 

approaches in each CIRCuIT city cluster. Strong policy and auditing processes were shown 

to positively influence recycling rates. Stakeholder perspectives on the strengths and 

weaknesses of material reuse and recycling markets were presented in chapter 3. Practical 

perspectives from real demolition demonstrators and lessons learnt were presented in chapter 

5. 

CIRCuIT partners proposed and improved a CIRCuIT pre-demolition audit template based on 

practical experiences and lessons learnt. A checklist for the use of the CIRCuIT pre-demolition 

audit is presented in Appendix 1.  

Key lessons learnt from project partners are listed in the following section.  

 

 Key lessons learnt  

Pre-demolition audits  

1. Mandatory pre-demolition audits – Cities should consider mandatory audits for all 

buildings and infrastructure without size thresholds to increase reused/recycled 

material circulation. Potential incremental steps towards this include: 

a. The desktop study component of a PDA could become a condition of all 

demolition permits. 

b. PDAs could become mandatory for all government projects. 

c. Typology specific PDAs – local practitioners to adjust and share PDA templates 

specific to local construction types to make future PDAs more efficient.  

2. Pre-demolition audit timelines – PDAs should be triggered by the forecast building 

end of life stage and should be integral in planning for how the site is used and 

designed in the future, rather than just for the demolition itself. Ideally, the building 

owner could then provide the PDA to demolition contractors in the tendering process 

95 

This project has received funding from  

the European Union’s Horizon 2020  

research and innovation programme  

under grant agreement No 821201 



 

(see point 11) to enable offers that incorporate reuse and recycling activities and 

timelines. PDAs should be completed as early as possible to maximise reuse and 

recycling opportunities. 

3. Project stakeholder engagement – The PDA process should be performed via a 

participative approach to maximise knowledge sharing and to maximise 

reuse/recycling opportunities. A site visit should be completed with all key project 

stakeholders. 

4. Identifying reusable and recyclable elements – Pre-demolitions audits are often too 

focused on the detection of homogeneous waste fractions. The level of detail of 

reporting must shift to identify reusable and recyclable elements and components in 

order to facilitate recirculation. The CIRCuIT PDA template seeks to improve this.  

5. Circular targets – Results of the PDA should be used to set project circular targets 

including a clear distinction between reuse/recycling and downcycling. 

Demolition  

6. Industry training – Margins for improvement in selective demolition technologies and 

workforce skills are large. Lack of industry knowledge and/or experience regarding 

damage free disassembly leads to degradation of material qualities (function, strength 

and appearance) and severely reduces circular opportunities. Skilled and experienced 

contractors to guide circular processes are crucial.  

7. Manual handling and technological development – Time consuming manual 

handling is often required for high precision tasks such as removal and cleaning of 

building elements and components. This highlights the need for improved design for 

disassembly construction practices and the need to scale up innovative 

technology/equipment and approaches to avoid manual handling. 

8. Material deterioration – Deterioration of in use building materials after end of use but 

prior to demolition is a common issue. This includes vandalism, vermin, weathering 

and mould. This highlights the importance of completing PDAs early to trigger 

reuse/recycling processes. 

9. Material storage – A common limiting factor during demolition was material storage. 

Cities should consider policies that facilitate more material storage facilities (directly 

via providing facilities or indirectly through planning/economic incentives). Project 

leaders should consider material storage and recirculation during early planning, with 

onsite storage and onsite reuse/recycling where possible to avoid transportation.  

Material Circularity 

10. Data transparency – Improved material data transparency and material information 

improves market confidence, reduces risk and provides opportunities for long term

the European Union’s Horizon 2020  

research and innovation programme  

under grant agreement No 821201 



 

planning. Improved material information should facilitate improved quality assurance 

and consumer material choice to compete with virgin construction materials and enable 

more opportunities for closed loop reuse and recycling of materials (Business-to-

Business/Customer or Customer-to-Customer). The CIRCuIT pre-demolition audit 

template seeks to contribute to this with improved reporting detail regarding reusable 

elements, components and materials. 

11. Project administration and reuse operators – Time, cost, PDA (if already 

completed), circular targets and responsibility should be included in project 

administration from the outset through tender documentation and contracts to ensure 

that reuse/recycling opportunities are maximised. Circular opportunities are commonly 

missed due to unclear responsibilities, poor communication among project 

stakeholders and compressed timelines. The actual removal and transport of specific 

building elements to be reused and associated administration of recirculation were 

common areas lacking clear responsibility. This highlights the required expertise of 

reuse operators.  

12. Circular incentives – Incentives are required to drive industry change towards 

material circularity and to encourage more projects to implement circular targets via 

PDAs. 

13. Material exchange platforms – The need for centralised platforms for industry to 

share, network and exchange materials for reuse is growing. A strong local platform is 

crucial to enable material recirculation.  

14. Digitised planning processes – Digital planning permit application processes (via for 

example BIM models, digital building logbooks and building passports) would help 

build up a citywide material databank in the long term and improve the accuracy of pre-

demolition audits.

 Implementation  

This section describes potential ways to implement the lessons learnt and the CIRCuIT PDA 
template in partner cities in the future.  

 

6.2.1 Helsinki region 

The city of Vantaa has joined the national Sustainable Demolition Green deal during the 

CIRCuIT project. The city is committed to conduct PDAs in all its future demolition projects. In 

addition to that, the city will share PDA information in an open database that is being 

developed as part of the national platform ecosystem including material exchange portals. 

The city of Vantaa has set a strategic target to be a frontrunner in circular construction. To 

support this target, it is developing its procurement practises both in demolition of buildings 

and construction of new buildings as well as refurbishment of buildings. The circularity criteria 

for the procurement of these different kind of construction categories are developed in 

conjunction with each other.  

The procurement criteria for circular construction are being developed in close cooperation 

within three major cities in Helsinki area, Helsinki, Espoo and Vantaa. The cities want to 

engage all important stakeholders in this process. The first market dialogue event will be held 

in January 2022 with the theme of demolition projects. The cities are aiming to set the targets 

in their demolition projects in a way that the market can respond. 

 

6.2.2 Copenhagen  

Implementing a circular pre-demolition audit on building or renovations projects in Denmark is 

not mandatory. Screening and/or performing audit for hazardous substances are mandatory, 

but no regulations do yet demand building owners to perform a full pre demolition audit. Thus, 

the implementation of complete pre-demolition audits is still happening on a voluntary basis in 

Denmark. 

Therefore, municipalities can help push this agenda and take the lead. In the City of 

Copenhagen, there is an increasing awareness of the necessity and benefits of circular 

construction, and it is a part of the increasing political focus on circular economy in the 

municipality’s building projects.  

In 2019, a proposal where reuse and recycling of construction materials are to be included in 

public tenders and in the Copenhagen Waste Management Plan 2024 was considered by the 

Finance Administration who is the entity responsible for the building projects in the 

Copenhagen municipality. The proposal was then accepted by the Copenhagen City Council. 

Furthermore, it was proposed and approved by the City Council in 2020 that conducting a 

DGNB-certification is required in the municipality’s building and renovation processes of larger 

entities.

Performing pre-demolition audits is still not happening in all of the city’s construction or 

renovation projects, but as a part of implementing the above-mentioned political proposals, 

the municipality has been working with pre-demolition audits in selected pilot projects, for 

example Jacob Michaelsens Minde, which is a demonstrator in the CIRCuIT-project. 

The Copenhagen municipality is currently evaluating how to incorporate pre-demolition 

guidelines into future practises. The City is analysing the lessons learnt and the tools 

developed in the CIRCuIT project in order to evaluate which approaches are most effective in 

a Copenhagen context and improves the circularity in buildings in the best way. 

An important consideration is how to implement the pre-demolition audit into the tendering

contribute to making the tender material increasingly circular. 

It is a hope, that if the City of Copenhagen together with other Danish municipalities and other 

private actors within the building industry with a strong sustainability focus such as pension 

companies take the lead and demonstrate how to perform circular demolition and construction. 

This can help push the market towards circular construction and increase the demand for 

circular building materials also in the private building sector.  

In conclusion, the legislation and political incentives in Copenhagen is prepared to increase 

circularity in the municipality’s building portfolio, now it needs to be fully implemented step by 

step. 

 

6.2.3 Hamburg  

If PDAs were to become mandatory in Hamburg, the desk study phase of the PDA could 

initially become part of the application for a demolition permit. If no building size threshold 

were to apply, the PDA would have to be easier to use via pre-populated templates that could 

be submitted via an online portal. Such a portal could link data with a material exchange portal. 

The templates could list the most common components, elements and materials for different 

construction types.

6.2.4 London 

 

Earlier engagement with demolition contractors 

London demolition contractor feedback highlighted that pre-demolition audits provide more 

confidence in reuse and recycling planning (quantities, on and off-site options, required 

haulage) because there is a better understanding of what is in the building. It was indicated 

that earlier involvement in projects provides more time for this waste management planning. 

Planning is important to better facilitate deconstruction and segregation, such as ensuring 

access to specialised equipment and storage space. It is also provides more time to identify 

off-takers for reusable components, especially when working with bespoke component 

designs. Access to more accurate and digital building information also enables better planning.

Sustainability strategies and managers within demolition organisations  

For the London demonstrators, involved demolition contractors employ a sustainability 

manager, who drives their reuse and recycling initiatives. They also have short term strategies 

(up to 5 years) for increasing the incorporation of circular economy principles in their 

operations. This approach includes encouraging circular economy thinking for clients by 

providing circular solutions for tenders, even when not requested. It also includes internal 

training and pilot projects to improve knowledge of and approaches to deconstruction and 

segregation. 

Expansion of policy interventions 

The new London Plan 2021 requires referable applications (for planning permission of 

potential strategic importance) to promote circular economy outcomes and aim to be net zero-

waste. A Circular Economy Statement should be submitted, which includes a mandatory 

requirement for pre-demolition audits. The London Plan approach could be cascaded to Local 

Plans (municipality level) to be reflected in a larger amount of new developments. It could also 

be expanded to stand-alone demolitions that may not be captured in redevelopment 

applications.  

Increased material specific reclamation guidance and training 

London demonstrators explored reuse and higher value recycling for metals and timber. For 

metals, relocation and remanufacture were considered the most effective strategies in the 

demonstrator as they would maintain the value of the component in its second use. Relocation 

could be done at building, component or element level. However, these approaches are not 

yet common practice. For timber it was found that it did not take longer to remove for 

reclamation in comparison to normal procedures. Whilst this finding is likely to vary across 

projects, it could be an area of further exploration for demolition contractors to facilitate 

increased reuse/remanufacture over downcycling. Manufacturers could also provide advice 

on preferable reclaimed timber specifications. London’s city cluster aims to encourage 

increased timber segregation towards increased reclamation potential by publishing the 

results of the timber case study along with advice on visual inspection.
