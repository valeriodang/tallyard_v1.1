Executive Summary 

Overview  

The aim of this report is to contribute to the sustainable management of materials arising from the 
proposed demolition of 322 High Holborn, 44 Southampton Buildings and Holborn Gate, London. 

Methodology 
Information supporting the audit was gathered from architectural drawings and reports supplied by 
the client, as well as non-invasive observations and measurements carried out by Reusefully Ltd on 
10th July 2024. Where supporting information could not be gathered through these sources, 
assumptions were made based on typical scenarios for the building typology.  

The gathered information was analysed to derive the estimates of materials arising, as well as the 
potential opportunities for reuse, recycling, and landfill diversion.

1. The Requirement 

Gleeds Management Services engaged Reusefully Ltd to carry out a pre-demolition audit of High 
Holborn, London WC1. The aims of the audit are:  

• To provide an understanding of the types and amounts of products and materials arising 
during the refurbishment/demolition to enable reuse in the new development or elsewhere 

• To optimise the management of products and materials and provide recommendations to 
the design team and demolition contractor in line with the waste hierarchy i.e. maximise 
reuse and recycling and minimise waste to landfill 

• To provide technical advice on the reuse of products and recycling of material on and off-site 

• To facilitate better links and communication within the supply chain and provide details of 
local charities, organisations and companies who may be able to deal with the reuse and 
higher value recycling of products and materials arising 

• To provide data to help with populating the Resource Management Plan and in support of 
any assessment e.g. BREEAM Wst01 and GLA’s planning requirement 

• To advise on targets for reuse and recycling for products and materials  

• Mitigation of negative effects from demolition.   

 

The audit follows the requirements as set out in the Code for Pre-Demolition Audits2 and the Mayor 
of London’s Circular Economy Guidance (March 2022)3, and is fully compliant with the BREEAM 
Wst01 criteria.  A Pre-redevelopment Audit Report was issued by Reusefully Ltd in June 2023.

2. Existing Buildings 

The existing site contains three buildings arranged around a central courtyard though not connected  
internally, with some associated external space around the periphery. For the purposes of this  
assessment, there are three distinguishable ‘zones’ to the site, based on construction date, typology  
and position of the buildings – these are highlighted in the site plan and aerial view of the site below,  
and are described in further detail in the subsequent sections. The zones are:  
 

• Holborn Gate (outlined in yellow)  
• 322 High Holborn (outlined in blue)  
• 44 Southampton Buildings (outlined in red)  

 

 

 
 

 

 

322 High Holborn is an eight-storey commercial building dating from the late 1960s, comprising 
retail space and a reception/lobby on the ground floor, six floors of offices above, several residential 
units on the 8th floor, and a basement. Access between storeys is achieved through a single access 
and services core to the rear of the building, as well as a lift to the Western end which provides 
direct access to the residential units.   

The internal spaces have been refurbished at least once since construction. Access was not possible 
to all floors at the time of the site visit. Floor to ceiling heights is low (ranging from 2.6m typically to 
2.3m under chilled beams).     

Holborn Gate. Similarly to 322 High Holborn, Holborn Gate is an eight-storey building dating from 
the early 1960s, with retail space and a reception/lobby on the ground floor, six floors of offices

between storeys. The building is larger than 322 High Holborn, extended away from the street

44 Southampton Buildings was built as purpose-built offices in 1913-1914, and features a ground 
floor with reception and lobby, four floors of office space, a basement with meeting rooms and end-

3. Development Proposals  

The proposed scheme is for a total demolition of the 3 existing buildings on site and a new 
development of a grade A office building with retail space on the ground floor and supporting 
facilities in one basement.  

A comprehensive technical review was undertaken by the design team as part of the optioneering 
process. This review included a thorough assessment of the existing buildings to determine the most 
viable approach for the project. The results demonstrated the benefits of the new build option 
compared to the other WLCA options. 

It is important to note that at the start of the optioneering process, the various disciplines involved 
completed qualitative considerations at the building level. These considerations were presented to 
the City of London (COL), ensuring that all potential options were evaluated thoroughly.

Analysis  

On the basis of information gathered and provided, a full analysis of materials arising from the 
proposed demolition has been undertaken, with results reported in both weight and volume, which 
have been calculated using standard density figures for the materials identified.  Areas are also 
provided where useful. The methodology has followed the BRE Code of Practice for Pre-
Redevelopment Audits (July 2017)4. 

Assumptions can be found in Appendix B.  

Note for the purposes of this report, reuse is defined as reusing a product/material with minimal 
reprocessing usually in the same or similar function.  Recycling is any recovery operation where 
waste materials are reprocessed into products, materials or substances, whether for original or 
other purposes. For example, crushing aggregates is considered as recycling activity. This report 
recommends for each material/product reuse and recycling options as per the value retention 
hierarchy (see below).

opportunities, estimated quantities available for reuse, and considerations for reuse. A high-level

embodied carbon data – as such the actual embodied carbon that could be saved is likely greater 
than is reported here.  

Overall, we estimate that approximately 664 tonnes of arisings could be reused, corresponding to

Reuse focus: Raised access floor panels  

Raised access flooring is present throughout the majority of office floors and circulation spaces 
throughout the site, covering an area of around 17,780m2. Accounting for incomplete pieces (eg 
edge pieces or panels with service apertures), as well as panels in poor condition or otherwise not 
meeting requirements for reuse, we estimate that around 70% of the panels are reusable. This 
corresponds to approximately 12,446 m2 (or 34,572 panels) available for reuse, totalling around 321 
tonnes. If reused and displacing new raised access flooring, this would correspond to avoided 
embodied carbon emissions (modules A1-A3) of approx. 281 tCO2e.   

The type, condition and grade of the RAF panels are assumed to be suitable for reuse (ie medium 
grade, more modern steel skin and timber core, no damages). Only a small quantity was directly 
observed, however it is assumed that most RAF panels meet requirements based on the use type 
and likely timeframe in which they have been refurbished.   

Raised access flooring featuring a chipboard core with a steel skin 
is now well-established as a reusable item due to its modularity, 
standard dimensions, and durability, as long as the panels are of a 
medium duty grade and are relatively modern and in good 
condition. Pedestals are not possible to reuse at present. 

An avenue for reuse of the panels is via RMF13, who test and 
certify reclaimed panels and resell them as part of their Eco range.  

To sell panels to RMF, pictures of the panels showing the side edge, under structure under side and 
any markings/stripes should be sent to info@rmf-services.co.uk or 07775 237247. If they are 
suitable for reuse, RMF will offer a price per panel and will collect. Any carpet will need to be 
removed and the panels kept dry and inside the building. RMF supply the pallets, banding and pump 
truck. They should be 32 no, per pallet, stacked face up, square and straight and banded tightly. 
Panels need to be in condition, any edge damage and rust is not acceptable.

7. Maximising reuse and high-value recycling 

General best practice 

It is advised that a long lead-in time as possible and maximum exposure are required to enable the 
reuse of products and components. The best chances for reuse, with the associated environmental 
and economic benefits, are as near to site as possible: 

• Used by the same client locally 

• Sold or given away locally 

Aside from the specific recommendations provided in Table 3, the following recommendations may 
assist in maximising the reclamation potential of the items identified (note: Reusefully can assist 
further with all of these activities): 

• Consider setting aside storage on site for segregation of salvaged items. 

• Work with the design team for the new development, consider any options for direct onsite 
reuse. 

• Consider options for reuse of items in any other assets or projects the client owns / is 
involved with. 

• Explore reuse and high-value recycling opportunities beyond the site and client, through the 
direct networks of the client, design team and construction team, and/or by posting to 
online reuse marketplaces (see further information below). At present, reuse primarily 
occurs through word-of-mouth and direct contacts.   

• Set out requirements and expectations for strip-out/demolition contractors in tenders 
relating to the methods and quantities of items targeted for reuse and recycling – this may 
include a requirement to meet certain targets for reuse and recycling, as well as ‘softer’ 
requirements such as an obligation to provide method statements for how reuse and high-
value recycling will be addressed.  

• Make allowances for additional cost and programme implications of recovery beyond 
business-as-usual, in particular in the strip-out/demolition/enabling works contract. 

• Carry out or commission ‘resource tracking and management’ of items targeted for reuse 
and recycling beyond BAU – this enables greater planning, implementation, and verification 
of actions that support circular management of each item. More detailed schedules and 
datasets may be required for target items to ensure optimal management, for instance a 
schedule of the different door types, sizes and conditions can help designers in the new 
scheme specify the ones that match their design intent or modify their design accordingly.  

• Consider logistical aspects beyond just the demounting of salvaged items at an early stage, 
such as testing, transport, and storage.

Having an appropriate location to store reusable items is a major enabler of onsite reuse and can

offsite storage.  

Careful planning is required to ensure that items are stored in such a way to avoid damage and 
deterioration, whilst also being placed in a location that creates minimal interference with works 
onsite and no unacceptable health and safety issues (eg relating to fire or structural loading). It may 
be that certain features must first be stripped out or demolished first before a space can be used for 
storage, as long as these features were due to be removed in any case. For instance, if the basement 
internal walls are to be removed, it would be beneficial to prioritise this to enable items from the 
soft strip to be stored and easily accessible due to the more open basement layout.

10. Mitigation of demolition  

There are a number of ways to mitigate the effects of demolition. This includes: 

• Retention of any elements is preferred, reducing the impacts of the materials becoming 
waste.  

• Reuse of materials and products as recommended. This would save up to 280t/CO2e. Any 
reuse of products from the existing building would also reduce the embodied carbon of the 
new scheme.  

• There may be options to donate materials to the local community/charities, particularly any 
furniture, white goods etc. These can also be donated via Reuse Organisations/Networks.  

• Higher value recycling options should be investigated, such as recycling glass back into glass, 
which offers considerable carbon savings 

• The distances materials/waste travels to its destination is a factor, it may sometimes be 
better to crush materials onsite rather than transport them large distances.  

• Evidence should be asked from the demolition contractor(s) on how they will mitigate any 
environmental impact on site through their operations. This could be through their energy 
and fuel choice (e.g use of HVO).  

• Use of technology to minimise dust and other pollutants.

Most post-consumer flat glass waste produced does not go back into glass and is used as aggregate

contaminated.

• Class C – which is contaminated and not suitable for re-melting back into glass.

For recycling, metals should be segregated from other materials on site. For instance, rebar can be 
separated from the concrete it is bonded by using a crusher to pulverise the concrete into aggregate 
and sand, and then extracting the rebar for segregation.  

It is common practice for demolition contractors to reduce their contract value by allowing for the 
income from the recycling of metals during demolition. Standard skip hire companies are likely to 
charge for haulage costs only and may give back a small rebate on the metals. Once segregated, it is 
usually sent to a metal scrap merchants (recyclers). At these, the metals will be sorted, sheared 
(cutting large pieces), shredded, graded, and baled. The steel will be then sent to smelters to be re-
melted as ingots (which are usually downcycled material), and then sent to steel furnaces. Much of 
this may be abroad - depending on the price per tonnes the scrap merchant can obtain.  

Best practice for steel is for it to be reused; recycling is the business-as-usual model. Celsa Steel29 are 
introducing a scheme where steel can be bought by them and recycled in their furnace and a 
voucher provided for new high recycled content steel (around 98%) (mainly rebar). They are looking 
for companies to work with.
