Executive Summary 

This report presents the findings of a pre-redevelopment audit exploring the potential to retain 
existing built assets and associated infrastructure (e.g. public realm, ancillary spaces, etc.) on the 
site, in line with the Circular Economy Statement Guidance as required for developments referable 
to the GLA under the London Plan Policy SI 7.  

The pre-redevelopment audit is intended to help clients determine whether retention of the existing 
building is feasible whilst still delivering project aims and wider societal benefit, thus avoiding the 
waste, material use, and embodied carbon emissions generally associated with new construction.  

See Appendix A for details of the report authors.  

Several options for avoiding full demolition of the existing assets were evaluated for feasibility. The 
audit found that whilst it may be technically feasible to retain some assets (e.g. in terms of capacity 
of the structure to tolerate proposed activities), there may be challenges relating to commercial 
viability, and the social value of retention scenarios may be limited. A summary is provided on the 
following page, and more detailed analyses can be found in Section 4.  

Note that in any retention scenario, demolition of the existing building cores and construction of 
new cores would be required for compliance with fire requirements, resulting in significant 
demolition and construction works.  

The evaluations provided in this report are high-level and indicative only. Where retention is given 
further consideration for any of the assets, it is essential that the client commissions further 
assessments by suitably qualified professionals (e.g. structural engineers, commercial real estate 
analysts) to ensure that the retained assets would be safe, fit-for-purpose, and would demonstrate 
suitable performance and condition. Section 5 provides a summary of the recommended 
assessments.

1. The Requirement 
Gleeds, working on behalf of Hoi Hup Realty Pte Ltd, engaged Reusefully Ltd to carry out a pre-
redevelopment audit of existing buildings at Holborn Gate, 322 High Holborn, and 44 Southampton 
Buildings in London to understand whether retention of the existing buildings was feasible whilst 
meeting client aims and achieving wider social, economic and environmental benefits. 

This investigation supports compliance with the requirements of the London Plan Policy SI7 and the 
complementary London Plan Guidance for Circular Economy Statements (March 2022 revision) 1. For 
eligible projects in London, the Circular Economy Statement Guidance states that a pre-
redevelopment audit should be undertaken to determine the potential for full or partial building 
retention (see Figure 1, below).

2. Methodology 
Reusefully assessors visited the High Holborn site on 1st June 2023, during which a non-invasive 
visual survey of existing assets was undertaken. Observational notes and photographs were taken to 
help determine the existing use patterns, dimensions, layout, condition, performance, and degree of 
adaptability of the building. Access was possible to most areas of the site and buildings.  

The client supplied Reusefully with images of original section and plan drawings, as well as details of 
options being considered for the proposed project on the site.  

This information, as well as publicly available information such as EPC records, was used to 
undertake an evaluation of the feasibility of retaining existing assets on the site.   

When evaluating the feasibility of each scenario for each building, several factors were considered, 
including:  

• Technical capacity of the asset to accommodate retention scenarios 

• Potential energy performance if retained and in-use, after any necessary fit-out, 
refurbishment, retrofit, or repair work is completed.   

• Potential occupant comfort if retained and in-use, after any necessary fit-out, 
refurbishment, retrofit, or repair work is completed.  

• Construction practicalities and logistics, i.e. whether the practical aspects of retaining the 
asset (in combination with any other site works, e.g. construction of other new assets) are 
likely to be prohibitively difficult or expensive.  

• Social and economic value considerations, i.e. whether the existing asset would offer any 
significant benefit to the social and economic wellbeing of the wider community if retained.  

• Commercial viability of the retention scenario. 

Please note this report is not a full condition survey, nor does it include any structural assessment.  
As already noted, the findings are based on visual assessment and dialogue with the architects for 
different scenarios.

The existing site contains three buildings arranged around a central courtyard though not connected

3.1 322 High Holborn 

322 High Holborn is an eight-storey commercial building dating from the late 1960s, comprising 
retail space and a reception/lobby on the ground floor, six floors of offices above, several residential 
units on the 8th floor, and a basement. Access between storeys is achieved through a single access 
and services core to the rear of the building, as well as a lift to the Western end which provides 
direct access to the residential units.  

The internal spaces have been refurbished at least once since construction and are of a varying 
quality (reasonable overall), mostly in good condition, though access was not possible to all floors at 
the time of the site visit. Floor to ceiling heights are low (ranging from 2.6m typically to 2.3m under 
chilled beams), however daylighting was mostly good owing to mainly open layouts and the quantity 
of glazing relative to the narrow floor plates.    

Externally, however, the building offers little in terms of architectural or heritage value and is of a 
low visual quality as described in the Pre-Application Historic Building Report for Hoi Hup Realty Pte 
Ltd (dated May 2023).  

It was not possible to evaluate the condition or remaining service life of the building services, 
however it can be assumed to be approaching end-of-life and require replacement in a retention 
scenario, given the age of most equipment.

3.2 Holborn Gate 

Similarly to 322 High Holborn, Holborn Gate is an eight-storey building dating from the early 1960s, 
with retail space and a reception/lobby on the ground floor, six floors of offices above, and two 
basement levels. Three cores provide access and delivery of building services between storeys. The 
building is larger than 322 High Holborn, extended away from the street frontage onto High Holborn. 
Design features, quality, and condition of the building and systems are essentially identical to that of 
322 High Holborn both internally and externally. The energy performance of the building appears to 
be mostly poor based on the available EPC records (which cover individually-let spaces).

3.3 44 Southampton Buildings 

44 Southampton Buildings was built as purpose-built offices in 1913-1914, and features a ground 
floor with reception and lobby, four floors of office space, a basement with meeting rooms and end-
of-trip facilities (e.g. bicycle store). According to the Pre-Application Historic Building Report (May 
2023), the building’s stripped classical façade on the street-facing elevation holds modest 
architectural/heritage value.  

Internally, fit-out is of a varied but overall reasonable quality, and all features appeared to be in 
good condition. The building appeared to be reasonably well-utilised, though some offices were 
unlet at the time of the site visit, according to the building personnel. The overall performance of the 
building in terms of internal environmental quality is unknown. Good daylighting and visual comfort 
was observed in most areas during the site visit, though these attributes were lacking somewhat in 
the basement-level meeting rooms. The energy performance of the building appears to be mostly 
poor based on the available EPC records (which cover individually let spaces).  

Several extensive works were undertaken on the building throughout the 20th century and in the 21st 
century, including an extension to the rear to house a new access core, a roof extension, and some 
alterations to the street-facing façade.  

The building footprint is located just within the boundary of the Chancery Lane Conservation Area, 
to which the façade makes a modest positive contribution according to the Pre-Application Historic 
Building Report. Remnants of what is believed to be the 12th-century Temple of the Knights Templar 
have been found under the northwest corner of the building.

4. Analysis  
Various factors were considered in evaluating the viability of retaining the existing buildings, and 
these are discussed in the following subsections. Against each factor, an evaluation is provided of 
whether or not it is believed to be prohibitive of retention (i.e. whether or not it presents 
justification against retention).  Note, these factors are considered for all of the retention scenarios. 

The factors taken into consideration included:  

• Technical capacity of the asset to accommodate retention scenarios 

• Potential energy performance if retained and in-use, after any necessary fit-out, 
refurbishment, retrofit, or repair work is completed.   

• Potential occupant comfort if retained and in-use, after any necessary fit-out, 
refurbishment, retrofit, or repair work is completed.  

• Construction practicalities and logistics, i.e. whether the practical aspects of retaining the 
asset (in combination with any other site works, e.g. construction of other new assets) are 
likely to be prohibitively difficult or expensive.  

• Social and economic value considerations, i.e. whether the existing asset would offer any 
significant benefit to the social and economic wellbeing of the wider community if retained.  

• Commercial viability of the retention scenario.  

Note that two factors – ‘social value considerations’ and ‘commercial viability’ – are not evaluated in 
terms of whether they are prohibitive of retention, but instead whether they are supported 
positively by the scenario or not. This difference is highlighted because these factors reflect wider 
ambitions –area-level social and economic outcomes and client financial performance – rather than 
the actual feasibility of the building being retained. These factors are also intrinsically linked with 
other factors such as occupant comfort, utilisation, and energy performance.   

4.1 322 High Holborn  

Technical capacity of building to accommodate retention scenarios: Not likely to prohibit retention 

No issues regarding the safety or longevity of the structure were made known to the assessors. 
Continued use, or adaptation to a use without significantly greater loads on any individual structural 
member, is not likely to present any issues. Where proposed adaptations, refurbishments, or 
expansion (combined with partial demolition if necessary) are likely to result in significantly modified 
loads, a structural engineer should be employed to model the tolerances under the proposed 
scenarios.  

However, conversion to other alternative uses such as retail (beyond the ground floor) or light 
industrial is likely not possible due to the site location, building access, and internal layouts and 
dimensions.

Partial demolition will be required to make the cores complaint with the latest fire requirements. If 
the residential units are retained, or new residential units are added, an additional core(s) will be 
required to make it compliant with the latest fire requirements.  

The potential for major changes to physical architecture, e.g. vertical extension, horizontal 
extension, or partial demolition, is unknown from the perspective of structural tolerances – this may 
be investigated further by a structural engineer. However, based on precedent it is conceivable that 
a roof extension (possibly lightweight) could be added, and/or that the building could be connected 
horizontally to any newly construction buildings. Note that floor heights between the buildings do 
not match up exactly, and this is likely to be a limitation on retention scenarios involving internal 
connection of the buildings (including where connected through horizontal extensions).  

Potential energy performance: Not likely to prohibit retention 

Currently poor (most offices in the building for which an EPC could be found have ratings of C or D) 
but are likely to be able to be brought to acceptable standard through measures such as insulation 
of the building envelope and thermal bridges, installation of higher-performance windows and 
doors, improved airtightness, damp-proofing, low-carbon heating, onsite solar panels, et cetera. 
Note the current performance and longevity of the building services is not known.  

Whilst these measures are likely to be extensive for the building to meet future building regulations, 
they do not prohibit retention of the existing building.  

Potential occupant comfort, health and wellbeing: Not likely to prohibit retention 

The office space and associated ancillary/amenity space features a high quality of fit-out in good 
condition, as well as plenty of daylighting. No data was received as regards air quality, thermal 
comfort, or other factors, however there is no reason to believe these aspects could not be rectified 
to a suitable degree if there are issues. As such, the building is likely to be suitable for continued use 
in its current state or modified (e.g. HVAC systems) if issues are identified. The same is true where 
the building is extended vertically. However, if horizontal extension is considered or the building or 
part thereof is to be incorporated into the new structure, design must allow for sufficient daylighting 
and provision of other aspects of comfort, health and wellbeing. The low floor to ceiling height in the 
existing building may impact on the provision of sufficient daylight should the depth of the floor 
plate be extended.  

In the context of meeting circular economy and reduced embodied carbon outcomes, non-
compliance with BCO guidance (or other guidance around quality) should not be seen as a solitary 
reason to demolish and replace an existing office building. If this were the case, a large portion of 
the current stock of office buildings would need to be replaced to comply with this guidance, and 
increasingly so as the guidance becomes more stringent, potentially decades before their planned 
technical life span is exhausted (typically 60 -80 years).  

Construction practicalities and logistics: Not likely to prohibit retention 

Retention of 322 High Holborn would make demolition of other structures and construction of any 
new structures on the site more difficult due to less space for equipment, vehicles, and materials.

However, access to the site is good and there is likely to be sufficient space. Established procedures 
for construction on sites with constrained space may be followed. As such this factor is not believed 
to be prohibitive of retention.  

Social value considerations: May be supported by retention  

Whilst the social and economic value of the building to the local and regional community are not 
likely to be especially high due to the lack of significant heritage/architectural value and the 
functionality of the buildings as primarily office space, the existing building does nonetheless provide 
significant floor area for workspace, supporting local and regional employment, attracting footfall to 
the local area, supporting the local economy and contributing to a sense of vibrancy.   

If retained, these benefits could continue to be delivered or may even be improved upon, for 
instance via:  

• Improvements to the architectural quality of external facades 

• Retrofit, refurbishment and upgrades to improve quality and performance of internal spaces 

• Provision of alternative building functions which may serve a greater socioeconomic need in 
the region, for instance residential space 

• Expansion of the buildings to provide more space of various types (and their individual 
merits).  

One scenario which social value may be supported by retention is the change of use from office to 
residential, either throughout the entire building or part thereof (e.g. 1 or 2 floors). Lack of 
residential supply is a strong factor in the current housing affordability crisis, which is especially 
pronounced in urban centres. Conversion to residential could help to alleviate this issue locally, 
whilst providing increased footfall to local business that have been affected by the transition to 
remote and flexible working. It is important, however, to consider conversion to residential on a 
case-by-case basis, accounting for whether an individual building is likely to be fit-for-purpose and 
provide a suitable level of amenity and comfort. Conversion to residential in this case would entail 
the construction of multiple new cores, and there may be issues with air quality and noise in street-
facing units (though this would not be unusual for city centre dwellings, and measures may be taken 
to alleviate issues to a certain extent).  

Overall, consideration should be given to the fact that the existing building does (or could, following 
refurbishment, adaptation, and/or expansion) deliver significant social and economic value in its 
own right. Although in some cases social value delivery may be of a lower magnitude than full new 
construction (though the opposite may be true in the case of change of use to housing), it would 
require significantly lower carbon, waste, resource consumption, and upfront cost, and would avoid 
disruption to the current tenants as well as the effects of construction on the local area (e.g. noise, 
vibration, traffic congestion).  

Commercial viability: May not be supported by retention  

Aside from the technical and functional aspects of retention feasibility, the overall feasibility of 
retaining a building (or part of a building) is affected by the commercial viability of different 
scenarios for retention versus demolition and new construction. The commercial viability is

intrinsically linked to the current technical performance of the building and the quality of the 
building(s).   

In the case of 322 High Holborn, there are issues with quality that may affect viability for commercial 
lettings, for instance the floor to ceiling heights, depths, and architectural aesthetics. As such, the 
potential for full retention with heavy refurbishment, with or without partial demolition and 
extensions/new construction, may be limited.   

According to building personnel as well as visual evidence, the building is fairly well utilised, with the 
majority of space tenanted and therefore providing a steady income. As such, there appears to be 
sufficient demand for the building. However, no evidence was received to corroborate this assertion.  

Whilst most of the building is currently in use as privately tenanted offices, the changing landscape 
of commercial real estate in London may mean that greater utilisation (in terms of average number 
of occupants using the building at any time) may be achievable through conversion to flexible office 
space and/or coworking. Alternatively, the space may be converted to alternative uses such as 
residential, the commercial viability of which cannot be foreseen at this stage without a full analysis.  

In any retention scenario, significant retrofit, refurbishment, and possibly partial demolition and 
extension/new construction, would be required. The cost of any such works must also be factored 
into the consideration of commercial viability. Even in a full retention scenario with no modification 
of use types or patterns (i.e. continued use as privately tenanted offices), the entire building would 
require extensive energy retrofit measures that could render the project unviable given the potential 
revenues.
