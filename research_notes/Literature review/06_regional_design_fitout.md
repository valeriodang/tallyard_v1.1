## D14 Circle Economy for Zero Waste Scotland: The Circularity Gap Report, Scotland

**Identity.** About November 2022, 2018 baseline, a national material-flow and scenario study. Authority medium to high: an established method using national data, but the scenarios are "what-if" and undated, and the report is not statutory.

**Summary.**
- Scotland is **1.3% circular**: 1.6 Mt of cycled materials out of 119.4 Mt consumed (L108-118, L441-455).
- Housing takes 20.3 Mt (17%) of consumption; 24.3 Mt (20.4%) a year is locked into new stock (L125, L872-905, L1754).
- Scenario 1, a circular built environment, cuts the footprint 11.2% and lifts the metric to 2.0% (L1660-1910). All scenarios combined reach 11.8% (L2802-2822).
- Recommendations: tax virgin materials, mandatory reuse targets, reuse in public procurement, digital platforms for secondary materials, and **"material hubs for deconstruction"** (L3100-3242, L4014).

**Key content.**
- **Metric:** secondary materials divided by total consumption. It is consumption-based and credits the country that uses the secondary material. It is mass-based and **measures neither value retention nor quality loss** (L814-1030).
- **Definition:** "Cycling: converting a material into a material or product of a higher (upcycling), similar (recycling) or lower (downcycling) embodied value and/or complexity." Energy recovery is not cycling (L316-331).
- **Flows:**
  - mineral waste recycling is "surprisingly low" at 17.4% (L1239-1243);
  - construction is 6.8% of the carbon footprint (L1519);
  - stone imports dominate construction imports (L2610-2611).
- **Scenario assumptions** (L1663-1748):
  - about 75% of construction and demolition waste is suitable for reuse;
  - reuse of steel, timber, doors and window frames is named explicitly;
  - retrofit of 41% of stock.
- **Decommissioning** (L2630-2760): about 110,000 t a year of steel from energy infrastructure against 163,000 t of construction demand, but **for recycling, not section reuse**.
- **Policy and bodies:**
  - Making Things Last; a Circular Economy Bill being drafted at the time;
  - the 2022 Route Map consultation, proposing best-practice standards and a "Scottish Programme for Reuse of Construction Materials and Assets" (L1768-1770);
  - NPF4 (L1759);
  - BE-ST, the Recycling Improvement Fund (£70m) and the Circular Economy Investment Fund (L4031-4036).
- **Skills** (L3690-3710): material scouts, deconstruction auditors, quality testers, procurement specialists.

**Informs Tallyard.**
- A Scotland requirement entry (see the synthesis).
- An authoritative three-way cycling definition for the decision tree.
- Its own admission that mass metrics ignore value supports Tallyard's value basis.
- Decommissioned infrastructure is a supply source.

**Contradictions.** "Cycled" lumps reuse, recycling and downcycling into one mass figure. The steel opportunity is recycling. Hubs are proposed, not existing.

**Gaps.** Weak end-of-life data; no component reuse targets, hub locations or carbon factors.

**Categories.** 1, 2, 8, 9, 11, 18.

## D51 West Midlands Combined Authority: circular economy routemap

**Identity.** By Useful Projects and SOENECS, January to August 2021. Covers 7 local authorities and 3 LEP areas (L427, L573-578, L5325). Authority medium: an official but non-statutory, consultant-written strategy, with interpolated material flows and internal inconsistencies.

**Summary.**
- Priorities: manufacturing, construction and food. Five enablers.
- Construction, demolition and excavation use 18 Mt of minerals (82% of the region's minerals). **4.5 Mt (58%) of their waste still goes to landfill** (L1983-2024, L4423-4453).
- Five construction interventions, including a **Zero Waste Construction Hub**: a physical and virtual hub to recover and exchange materials.

**Key content.**
- "Resource recovery and material exchange hubs are required" (L1392-1398). Select locations for regional resource recovery hubs and **"financially viable storage facilities"** (L1469-1470), then a network (L2963-2967).
- Hub KPIs: tonnes not landfilled or incinerated, and the material consumption ratio against traditional methods (L3099-3115).
- **Digital** (L2917-2938):
  - a kitemark for sharing platforms;
  - an interactive regional trading platform;
  - signposting to marketplaces, keeping business-led platforms separate from community-led ones;
  - **building passports with a West Midlands Building Material Bank**;
  - track and trace.
- **Proposed, not adopted** (L2716-2760, L1488-1550):
  - mandatory regional circular economy statements and demolition strategies, modelled on London;
  - Site Waste Management Plans re-introduced;
  - mandatory EPDs;
  - harmonised waste definitions "to simplify reuse, trading and transport";
  - ISO 20400 procurement.
  - Timeline: hubs about 2023, platform about 2024, statements about 2025, passports and material bank by 2041.
- "No significant policies in the region enabling circular economy at scale" (L1376-1377).
- Low-quality claim: construction waste worth up to £5bn a year at £1,000 per tonne (L2005-2007).

**Inconsistencies.** Recycled tonnage is 3.5 Mt in the text but 3.2 Mt in a figure; stone 1.2 Mt in one place against about 12 Mt implied elsewhere.

**Informs Tallyard.** A West Midlands requirement entry with proposed instruments marked as proposed. Hubs flagged as planned. Positions Tallyard as the virtual hub, trading platform and passport.

**Categories.** 2, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11, 17.

## D48 Baker-Brown: The re-use atlas (with the preview file)

**Identity.**
- RIBA Publishing, 2017, ISBN 978 1 85946 644 5 (L76-82). A practitioner book with case studies and essays.
- `preview-9781000700183_A37991192.txt` is identical to its first 798 lines.
- Authority medium: first-hand cases but qualitative.

**Summary.** Four steps: recycle, reuse, reduce, closed loop.
- Recycling grinds, melts or pulps waste "often into a less useful and second-rate new material" (L1927-1965).
- With reuse "the value can stay the same or on occasion it can increase" (L2813-2830).
- Building cases relevant to Tallyard:
  - Superuse Studios;
  - Hub 67 from Olympic remnants;
  - Rotor in Brussels;
  - Oslo Urban Mountain;
  - Lacaton and Vassal and Wilmcote House;
  - Brummen Town Hall and Alliander HQ with passports;
  - Venlo City Hall;
  - the Brighton Waste House.

**Key content.**
- **Greenfield's resource hierarchy** (L1569-1605): disassembly or refurbishment, then reuse for alternative uses, then closed-loop recycling, then recycling for alternative uses, then energy recovery, then landfill.
- "Superuse" means improving value beyond the original use, in effect upcycling (L3063-3076).
- **Design from stock:**
  - Waste House (L6512-6560):
    - unknown timber provenance means the engineer assumes the weakest grade, refined later;
    - "find material first and then think about whether it might be useful", which is "a completely different design process";
    - material arrives weeks or months early, so storage is needed.
  - Superuse cannot show the client the final building until materials are sourced (L3095-3103). Its Harvest Map searches for surplus near the site and avoids storage by ordering direct from demolishers: "you become a warehouse manager, which is a different job" (L3119-3127, L3575-3583).
  - Rotor inventories, reserves before strip-out, photographs and researches provenance, and reinstalls in the same building (L4570-4660).
- **Prices (Rotor):**
  - **a ceiling of about 50% of the new price** for anonymous contemporary materials, with a floor at recovery cost (L4672);
  - wood waste sells for a few euros a tonne, the same wood as flooring for €1,000 to €2,000 a tonne (L4757);
  - about 400 t salvaged in 2015 with four staff (L4589-4757).
- **Legal and commercial barriers** (Hub 67, L3334-3500):
  - nine steel frames reused;
  - a 500-page NEC contract cut to 75 pages;
  - a building counts as "temporary" for Part L only for up to two years;
  - no certificates or performance data, so the risk sat on the designer's and contractor's insurance, which "reinforces the need for material passports".
  - UK VAT: 20% on refurbishment, zero on new build (L6362-6368).
- **Case figures:**
  - Oslo: 90% of strip-out recycled or reused; 50% of facade glass reused as partitions (L3894-3960).
  - Alliander: 90% of materials kept on site, 50% reused (L5461-5497).
  - Venlo: a supplier guarantees to **buy back the fit-out at 18% of its value after 10 years** (L5588-5603).
  - Brummen: timber cut to common sections for future reuse; bolted, not welded (L5381-5405).
- **Digital vision:** BIM models of existing buildings with passports, and an online "material flow market" that announces flows as they arise and avoids storage (L7568-7600).

**Informs Tallyard.**
- A design-from-stock workflow with reservation and options.
- A pricing heuristic.
- Upcycle and downcycle definitions.
- Passport contents (specification, toxicity, location, function, dismantlability; L5303-5310).
- Risk and insurance notes.

**Contradictions.** Hub-only logistics: direct site-to-site transfer and reinstalling in the same building are major routes. Reuse does not always save money (L2993-3005).

**Categories.** 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, 10, 11, 13, 15, 16, 17, 18.

## D49 Ressource Blokken: upcycling 1960s and 1970s social housing (Danish)

**Identity.**
- GXN (3XN's research arm), JAJA Architects, Teknologisk Institut, Regnestuen and SBi, funded by Realdania, March 2021 (L1-32).
- Authority medium to high: real lab testing and SBi life-cycle figures, but only one flat sampled per building.

**Summary.**
- Danish law requires listed social-housing estates to cut family social housing to 40% by 2030: 1,360,300 m2 is due for demolition and 724,300 m2 of new homes (L40, L105-139).
- The study mapped three blocks in Esbjerg and Odense (from 1967). Precast floor decks and walls carry most of the mass and embodied carbon. Direct reuse saves most of it, but the old concrete is structurally downgraded.

**Components.** Hollowcore decks (180 mm), in-situ decks and walls (150 mm), room-sized box units, facade panels, brick cladding, asbestos-cement panels, windows, doors, steel columns, stairs, terrazzo, parquet.

**Tests** (L320-425):
- strength 26.8 and 33.0 MPa, about class C20/25;
- carbonation 24 to 30 mm in one deck, **past the reinforcement**;
- cover close to the 10 mm limit;
- chloride low.

Consequences:
- passive indoor exposure only;
- unreinforced walls cannot be loadbearing in an equivalent building;
- shorter spans for decks;
- temporary bracing to lift;
- no outdoor storage.
- **"There are currently no paradigms for how concrete structures to be reused must be documented"** (L320-334).

**Hazards** (L265-310):
- Bricks can be reused outdoors; indoor use needs PCB removal or a risk assessment.
- Windows fail current energy requirements.
- Vinyl "must be destroyed"; asbestos-cement panels contain asbestos.
- A Danish three-way split: reuse, recycling, material recovery.

**Carbon:**
- Stengårdsvej: about 700 t CO2 in the concrete; cutting and disassembly is **12 to 16%** of that; net saving about 600 t (L185-262).
- Birkeparken: a new-build equivalent of 2,286 t; possible saving 1,732 t.
- Reusing each element three times saves 33 to 60% (L551).
- Danish demolition concrete is over 90% "used again", but mostly as road sub-base, which saves little CO2 (L991-1035).
- **No costs are given.**

**Design concepts:**
- **Preserve, then reuse, then upcycle.** Elements go to an on-site material bank, then into a hybrid glulam frame reusing about 95% of the precast elements (L480-580).
- Two-storey homes whose dimensions are set by the reused elements (L585-670).
- Hollowcore cut into cladding showing its round channels, on reversible brackets (L705-740).
- Facade panels cut into paving; crushed brick as terrazzo; reused double glazing as secondary panes.

**Process** (L920-990):
- Screening before the development plan.
- A "maximum reuse" strategy as a condition of demolition funding.
- "Reuse strategies must be considered from the beginning" (L1035).

**Informs Tallyard.**
- The strongest evidence for a **precast structural family**, with downgrade rules and test fields.
- Carbon for cutting and multiple cycles.
- A decision-tree order of retain, reuse, upcycle, recycle, recover.
- Screening by build era.

**Contradictions.** Tallyard's rule skips upcycle and has no "retain" option. High diversion can mean low value retention. Reuse is not zero-carbon.

**Categories.** 2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 15, 16.

## D13 Materials reuse evaluation process: example (2024)

**Identity.** A London practice's pre-redevelopment audit for the refurbishment of a luxury retail store, July 2024. Authority medium (a real practice method).

**Content.**
- Reuse inverts the usual design process, with materials dictating design. Site assessments record materials and how they are mounted, to estimate recovery rates. Early engagement with craftspeople and suppliers.
- Steps across RIBA stages:
  - stage 2: site visit and catalogue;
  - stages 3 to 4: explore design potential and engage suppliers;
  - stage 5: install and catalogue.
- The audit informs outcomes, the materials palette, reuse streams and waste routes, from a visual assessment and drawings.
- **Three criteria:**
  - **reuse potential**: retain, reconfigure (same use, new place), reimagine (new element), recycle;
  - **demountability**: extract whole, extract part, design for future disassembly;
  - **value**: quality, cost and time against carbon, and residual value.
- Each graded highly likely, complex (further investigation), or not suitable.
- **Item cards:** form, mounting, quantity, and reuse options (large slab, furniture, small tiles, aggregate). Examples: marble floor tiles probably laid on adhesive; backlit onyx on mechanical fixings; marble wall slabs; bronze vitrines; leather panels.
- **Mounting:** "threaded mechanical fixings are typically the easiest to remove", nails and friction fixings may damage, adhesives "can greatly impede reuse".
- **Stone hierarchy:** keep material in the largest pieces and as local as possible. Large slabs, then furniture, then smaller tiles, then chips. A worked example of reuse, upcycling and downcycling for one material.
- Next steps: measured quantities, specialist contractor engagement, a stage 2 decision, and design for disassembly in the new fit-out.

**Informs Tallyard.**
- An audit card format.
- Three scoring axes close to CIRCuIT's.
- Recoverability by mounting type.
- A concrete example for the upcycle and downcycle steps.
- Stage-based actions.

**Categories.** 6, 15, 16.

## D36 AHMM: Fit out, rip out, a study on the carbon cost of Cat A

**Identity.** AHMM (Allford Hall Monaghan Morris), version 1.2, March 2024, with input from London owners and agents. Authority medium (four case studies).

**Content.**
- Cat A fit-out in speculative offices is often ripped out within months by the incoming tenant's Cat B.
- Upfront carbon A1-A3 of four Cat A designs ranges from **58 to 92 kgCO2/m2**.
- **Raised access floor contributes 29 kgCO2/m2**, or 42 with a deeper void. A suspended ceiling is 16 kgCO2/m2.
- LETI's 2020 target is 600 kgCO2/m2, of which MEP and finishes are 104.
- Elements are classed **"at risk"** (likely ripped out: ceilings, lighting, terminal units) or **"baked in"** (raised floor, sprinklers, some ductwork).
- **RICS replacement rates:** raised floor 30 years, suspended ceiling 25, heating and air 20, cooling 15, ductwork 40, electrical 30, light fittings 15.
- **Lease lengths in the City of London (2019 to 2023):** 5.1 years for up to 10,000 sq ft, 8.7 years for 10,000 to 50,000, 13.2 years above 50,000. 80% of transactions are under 10,000 sq ft. That gives about eight tenants over 60 years, and up to 470 kgCO2/m2 of fit-out carbon over a building's life against the RIBA 2030 target of 750.

**Informs Tallyard.**
- Validates the raised floor carbon factor (40).
- A fit-out supply forecast from lease expiries.
- Families for raised floor, ceilings and luminaires.

**Categories.** 14, 8.

## Batch synthesis

### Top findings

1. **Outside London, hubs are planned or recommended, not running.** The West Midlands plans a construction hub, a recovery hub network with storage, a platform and passports; Scotland recommends deconstruction hubs. Model hubs with a status and allow a "virtual hub".
2. **Precast concrete is the largest uncovered family.** Direct reuse saves about 76 to 86% of the new-build equivalent, with downgrades to C20/25, indoor exposure only and shorter spans.
3. **The decision tree needs "upcycle" and arguably "retain".** The Scottish report, the Danish terms and Greenfield's hierarchy agree on the order.
4. **Design from stock is a distinct process:** find material first, let dimensions follow stock, reserve before strip-out, assume the weakest grade until tested, and plan storage.
5. **Mass metrics under-reward reuse.** Value-based reporting has support, but should sit beside mass.
6. **Testing and documentation are the missing link** for concrete, timber and ceramics.
7. **Commercial and legal frictions** to show as advisory notes: VAT, the "temporary building" rule, contract length, budget lines for leasing, and Rotor's pricing ceiling of about 50% of new.
8. **Both regional strategies call for digital platforms and passports.**
9. **Fit-out churn is a predictable supply stream.** City leases average 5.1 years for small offices; raised floor and ceilings carry significant carbon, and raised floor already has a recertification market.

### Indicative requirement entries (to check against current policy)

**Scotland**
- Strategy: Making Things Last (2016), Safeguarding Scotland's Resources (2013), the Zero Waste Plan (2010), and the 2022 Route Map consultation with a "Programme for Reuse of Construction Materials and Assets".
- Law: a Circular Economy Bill was being drafted at publication; it has since become the Circular Economy (Scotland) Act 2024. Verify.
- Planning: NPF4 encourages minimising waste and carbon. Net zero by 2045.
- Bodies and funds: Zero Waste Scotland, SEPA, BE-ST, the Circular Economy Investment Fund and the Recycling Improvement Fund.
- Targets: no construction-specific reuse targets in this document.
- Hubs: recommended only.
- Procurement: SPPN 3/2022 annual reporting (from D37).

**West Midlands**
- Strategy: the 2021 routemap; WM2041 net zero.
- Proposed instruments: mandatory statements and demolition strategies, EPDs, Site Waste Management Plans and ISO 20400 procurement.
- Infrastructure proposed: hubs, a platform and a material bank.

|---|---|---|

### Feature ideas, ranked
1. Precast concrete family with a downgrade engine: strength, carbonation against cover, chloride and exposure class flag "indoor only" or "reduce span".
2. Decision tree with retain, reuse, upcycle, recycle, downcycle, recover and dispose, and upcycle suggestions per family.
3. Design-from-stock workflow: stock-driven dimensions, reservation before strip-out, radius search, timeline with storage risk.
4. Testing-plan generator with "assume lowest grade" defaults.
5. Planned and virtual hub layer, plus direct site-to-site transfer.
6. Carbon for cutting and multiple reuse cycles.
7. Hazard screening by build era (PCB, asbestos, lead).
8. Passport export aligned with building passports.
9. Pricing guide (at or below 50% of new; floor at recovery cost) for donor owners.
10. Risk pack: Building Control, insurance, VAT and temporary-building notes.
11. Buy-back and take-back clause template.
12. Regional KPI report.
13. Fit-out supply forecast from lease expiries, and fit-out families (carpet tiles, ceilings, luminaires).
